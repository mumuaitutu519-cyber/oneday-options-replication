One-day bitcoin and ether options: What they know and what they cost

Abstract
Options that expire the next day now trade every day on bitcoin and ether. We ask what their prices know about next-day volatility and what a trader pays or earns for one day of variance. Across our sample, the one-day implied variance is more accurate than any model built on past returns, and far more accurate than the one-month implied volatility index. Across horizons up to one week, the best option-based forecast comes from the expiry that matches the horizon. Implied variance is above realized variance on most days, but a few very volatile days offset this premium on average, and at the exchange’s mark price the average premium is close to zero. A hedged seller earns less than the cost of trading, and one who trades at her own side of the spread loses before any fee. One-day option prices are informative and close to fair, and they support tighter risk limits for short-dated positions.

Highlights
• One-day option prices forecast next-day variance better than past returns.
• The best option forecast comes from the expiry that matches the horizon.
• The one-day variance premium is small and below the cost of trading.

1. Introduction
Options that expire within a day have become a large part of option trading. Option volume has grown quickly in recent years, led by retail investors (Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya, 2023), and much of the new activity sits in very short maturities. On Deribit, the main exchange for bitcoin and ether options, a new option series expires every morning, so a trader can always buy or sell an option with less than one day to run. These options are the natural tool for trading or hedging the risk of a single day. Their prices also carry the view of the market on how much the price will move before the next morning. A simple example shows what is at stake. Suppose that at 09:00, an at-the-money straddle that expires at 08:00 the next day costs 2 percent of the spot price. This price corresponds to an annualized volatility of about 50 percent. If the price then moves with a volatility of 40 percent until expiry, a seller who hedges the straddle keeps part of the 2 percent. If the price moves with a volatility of 80 percent, she pays out more than she received. Two questions follow. Does the 50 percent tell us more about the next day than the past behavior of prices does? And is the 2 percent too much, too little, or about right?
We answer both questions with every bitcoin and ether option trade on Deribit from January 2020 to October 2026. Each morning, we read the one-day implied variance from the at-the-money trades of the series that expires the next day. We compare it with the realized variance, which we compute from five-minute returns over the same 23 hours. We call the difference between the two the variance premium. We then follow a trader who sells the at-the-money straddle, hedges it every hour, and holds it to expiry, and we ask how much of the premium survives the prices at which she can actually trade.
We find four results. First, the one-day implied variance is the best single forecast of next-day realized variance over more than five years of evaluation that include the large moves of 2021 and 2022. Combined with past returns, it beats every model built on past returns, including machine learning models. The one-month implied volatility index, by contrast, forecasts one-day variance worse than past returns do. Second, the information is tied to the horizon. When we forecast the variance of the next one, two, three, or seven days, the most accurate option-based forecast always comes from the expiry that matches the horizon. Third, the variance premium is small. At-the-money implied volatility is above realized volatility on most days, but a few very volatile days offset these gains, and at the mark price of the exchange, the average one-day premium is close to zero in bitcoin and negative in ether. The positive premium known from longer maturities appears only when we add out-of-the-money options. Fourth, the premium is smaller than the cost of trading it. At the last traded price and before costs, the hedged seller earns a small amount in bitcoin and nothing in ether. At the prices on her own side of the spread, she loses in both markets even before fees. Risk limits built on the one-day implied variance hold up in backtests and, at the same coverage, need less capital than limits built on past returns in most cases.
Most of what is known about options that expire within a day comes from the stock index market, where such options now account for a large share of trading. Studies of that market ask whether a one-day volatility index improves forecasts of next-day variance (Albers, 2025), how much short-term option prices know about the distribution of returns over the next few days (Böök et al., 2025), how fast short-dated option prices absorb news (Willeboordse, 2026), and how a dealer hedges a position that expires within hours (Egebjerg, 2026). Three features of that market limit what it can say about the one-day variance premium. Index options expire at the close or the open of a trading day, so part of the period that the option covers is an overnight gap with no trading. The public record gives quotes or settlement prices rather than each trade with the mark price of the exchange attached. And a series that expires the next day has existed on every weekday only in recent years. Bitcoin and ether options on Deribit have none of these limits. The market trades through the night and the weekend, every trade carries the mark price at that moment, and a daily series has traded on nearly every day of our sample. We can therefore measure the one-day premium at traded, mark, and model-free prices on the same days, and compare it with the cost of trading at the prices a seller actually faces.
Our study adds to three lines of work. The first asks whether option prices forecast volatility better than past returns. Recent studies find that implied volatility improves forecasts of realized volatility in stock index markets (Branco, Rubesam & Zevallos, 2024; Chen & Li, 2023; Kambouroudis, McMillan & Tsakou, 2021), that short-dated equity options carry information that high-frequency returns miss (Todorov & Zhang, 2022), and that machine learning models beat simple time-series benchmarks (Christensen, Siggaard & Veliyev, 2023; Li & Tang, 2025), including for bitcoin (Huang, Sangiorgi & Urquhart, 2024; Wang, Rao & Zhuo, 2026). We show that in a market with daily expiries, one-day option prices beat both groups of models, and that the gain depends on matching the maturity of the option to the forecast horizon, which fits the view that the short end of the option surface measures current volatility (Aït-Sahalia, Li & Li, 2021; Todorov & Zhang, 2026). The second line measures the variance premium across maturities and the part of it that comes from tail events (Andries et al., 2025; Beason & Schreindorfer, 2022; Guo et al., 2023; Orłowski, Schneider & Trojani, 2024), and uses short-dated options to identify jump and tail risk (Bollerslev & Todorov, 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Chong & Todorov, 2024). We extend the term structure down to one day in a market without a close, measure it at traded, mark, and model-free prices, and show that the center of the option price is close to the variance that follows, while the tail part earns a small premium on days without jumps and gives it back on days with jumps. The third line studies the returns to selling options and the cost of trading them (Bali et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Nimalendran, Rzayev & Sagade, 2024; Zhan et al., 2022), together with the role of order flow in crypto option prices (Alexander et al., 2023). We show that one-day at-the-money options trade mostly at prices that sellers accept, and that the gap between these prices and the mark is as large as the premium itself. Cryptocurrency markets suit these questions because they trade without a close, so the realized variance has no overnight gap, and because the full record of trades is public (Liu & Tsyvinski, 2021). The next section describes the data and the measures, the following four sections examine what one-day options know, what one-day variance costs, what a hedged seller earns, and what the options imply for risk limits, and the last section concludes.

2. Data and Measurement
2.1 Data and sample
Our option data are all trades in bitcoin and ether options on Deribit. For each trade, the record gives the time, the price, the strike, the expiry, the futures price that the exchange uses as the underlying, the side that initiated the trade, and the mark price of the exchange at that moment.
Before 2020, the daily series rarely trades, so our sample starts in January 2020 and ends on October 6, 2026. We measure everything in a fixed morning window. The daily series expires at 08:00 UTC. Between 08:00 and 09:00, the series that expires the next morning becomes the shortest option, and at 09:00, it has exactly 23 hours left. We keep the trades in this window with a strike within 5 percent of the futures price in log terms, and we drop block trades and trades that belong to a multi-leg order. A day enters the sample when at least three such trades remain.

We also build three related measures. We do not winsorize the implied or realized variances, because the days with extreme realized variance are the ones that decide the average premium; Tables 3 and 5 report results without these days and with winsorized premia.

2.3 Descriptive statistics
Table 1 describes the sample. We have 2,422 days for bitcoin and 2,323 days for ether, and the median day has 271 and 192 at-the-money trades in the window, so the one-day implied variance rests on an active market rather than on a few stale quotes. The one-day implied volatility averages 52.7 for bitcoin and 65.1 for ether, close to the average realized volatility of 54.1 and 69.9. These levels are roughly three times the usual volatility of a broad stock index.
The two series differ much more in their spread than in their level. The standard deviation of realized volatility is about one and a half times that of implied volatility, and its maximum is about three times as large. This is what we should expect if the option price is a forecast. A forecast averages over the moves that might happen, so it varies less than the outcome, and the outcome includes jumps that no one could foresee. Bitcoin prices jump often (Hilliard & Ngo, 2022), and on such days the realized variance reaches many times its usual level while the implied variance set the morning before stays moderate. The lowest values of the variance premium in Panel B, about -600 volatility points for bitcoin and -880 for ether, come from such days.
Figure 1 plots monthly averages of the two series. They move closely together over almost seven years, through the boom of 2021, the collapse of 2022, and the calmer period after the launch of spot bitcoin exchange-traded funds in 2024. The largest gaps appear in the months with the sharpest price moves, when realized volatility is above implied volatility. A seller of one-day options therefore gains a little in most months and loses a lot in a few. The following sections put numbers on both sides of this trade.

3. What One-Day Options Know
3.1 Forecast models and evaluation
We now ask how well the one-day implied variance forecasts the realized variance of the same 23 hours. All forecasts are made at 09:00 on day t and use only information that is available at that time. We compare three groups of forecasts. The first group uses past returns. The baseline is the heterogeneous autoregressive (HAR) model, the standard benchmark in this literature (Clements & Preve, 2021), which we write as

where,  is the average realized variance over the k hours or days that end at 09:00 on day t, and  is the error. The last two terms let the model see the most recent hours, so that it has the same information as the option trades. We add, in turn, positive and negative semivariances (HAR-S; Bollerslev et al., 2022), a jump component (HAR-J), realized quarticity (HAR-Q), and a weekend indicator with the usual hour-of-week pattern of variance (HAR-C). The second group uses the 30-day index, either alone and scaled to 23 hours (IV30), or as an added regressor in equation (4) (HAR-IV30). We also fit three machine learning models on all of these variables, since such models can improve volatility forecasts: a lasso regression (LASSO), a random forest (RF), and a gradient-boosted tree (GB). Their tuning parameters are chosen each month on the most recent part of the estimation window only. We call all of these forecasts historical models, because none of them uses one-day options. The third group uses the one-day implied variance. We use it alone (IV), after a correction for the average bias of log IV in the estimation window (IV-C), and as an added regressor in equation (4) (IV+HAR).
We estimate every model on a rolling window of 500 days and estimate it again each day. A label enters the estimation window only after its 23 hours are complete. All models are compared on the days for which every forecast exists, from August 9, 2021, to October 5, 2026, which gives 1,522 days for bitcoin and 1,596 for ether and covers the large moves of 2021 and 2022. We measure accuracy with the QLIKE loss, which ranks forecasts correctly even when the realized variance is measured with noise,

We also run the conditional test of Giacomini and White, which leads to the same conclusions, and we report which models belong to the 90 and 75 percent model confidence sets, the usual tools in recent forecast comparisons.

3.2 Out-of-sample forecast performance
Table 2 reports the results. The one-day implied variance gives the most accurate forecasts in both markets. For bitcoin, the loss of IV-C is 13.5 percent below the loss of the HAR model, and for ether, the gain is 9.2 percent, both significant at the 1 percent level. For ether, the raw implied variance gains little over HAR, and the gain appears once the average bias is removed. The extensions of the HAR model change little, except that the hour-of-week pattern helps, which is in line with the calendar effects in intraday volatility reported by Zhang et al. (2024). The strongest historical model is LASSO for bitcoin and HAR-C for ether. Relative to these models, IV-C still lowers the loss by 5.3 percent for bitcoin and 4.9 percent for ether, although neither gain is significant. Once the one-day implied variance is added to past returns, the gain over the strongest historical model is 11.1 and 10.0 percent, both significant at the 1 percent level. For bitcoin, the 90 percent model confidence set contains only the three forecasts that use the one-day implied variance, and the 75 percent set contains only IV+HAR. For ether, the set is less selective, but it excludes the 30-day index at both levels. Combining the one-day implied variance with the GB forecast, on the 1,402 and 1,476 days for which this combination can be estimated, lowers the loss of GB by 13.1 and 13.8 percent.
These gains are large in economic terms. A variance forecast feeds directly into the hedge ratio of an option desk, the quotes of a market maker, and the margin that an exchange asks for. A forecast that is 13.5 percent more accurate means fewer days on which these numbers are set with a variance far from the one that follows. The gain is also large by the standards of the literature. In stock markets, machine learning models usually improve on the HAR model by a few percent, and flexible models rarely beat linear ones once implied volatility is among the inputs. A single option price, read once in the morning, does better here than all of our historical models, including the machine learning models.
The reason is the information set. Past returns can only describe how volatile the market has been. The option price also reflects what traders expect for the coming hours, such as scheduled macroeconomic news, large liquidations on leveraged venues, or news that has not yet moved the price. It also explains why the machine learning models gain little over the HAR model in our data. They use the same backward-looking inputs, and a more flexible function of the same past cannot know what traders know about the next day.
The 30-day index gives the opposite picture. Used alone, it is the worst forecast in the table, with a loss 37.9 percent above the HAR model for bitcoin and 28.7 percent for ether. The index is built from options that expire weeks later, so it reflects an average over a long period and misses what is specific to the next day. Theory points the same way. As the maturity of an option shrinks, its implied volatility moves toward the current spot volatility, while a 30-day option mixes the current state with the expected path of volatility over the following weeks. Scaling such a number down to one day keeps its average level but loses the daily signal. Many trading desks still use the 30-day index as their volatility input because it is the most quoted number in crypto, and our results show that this choice costs accuracy at short horizons.

3.3 Robustness and encompassing regressions
Panel A of Table 3 shows that these results do not depend on our choices. Columns (1) and (4) compare IV-C with HAR, columns (2) and (5) compare IV-C with the 30-day index, and columns (3) and (6) compare IV+HAR with GB, which is the machine learning model that past studies most often use. The first five rows change the estimation window. A 365-day window or an expanding window gives similar gains. The row Window 1,000 reports a 1,000-day window that leaves only the days after January 2024 for evaluation, and the next row applies the 500-day window to the same days. The gains are larger on these later days, and the two windows agree. The next six rows split the evaluation period. The gain over HAR comes from the period after 2022. It is 5.5 percent and not significant for bitcoin in 2021 and 2022, it is negative for ether in the same years, and for ether it is not significant before the launch of spot bitcoin exchange-traded funds in January 2024. The two other comparisons hold in every period: IV-C beats the 30-day index, and IV+HAR beats GB, in every row of the panel.
The timing of the gain has an economic reading. In 2021 and 2022, one-day options were a young market, and in 2022 prices were hit by a series of failures of large crypto firms that option prices set the morning before could hardly foresee. From January 2024, spot exchange-traded funds brought a large flow of new money into bitcoin (Hornback & Whaley, 2025). When bitcoin futures were first listed on regulated exchanges, the spot market became more efficient and less volatile (Augustin, Rubtsov & Shin, 2023), and Alexander et al. (2024) find that crypto option markets have become more efficient over time. A deeper market with more professional traders is consistent with option prices that know more about the next day, and the gain over HAR rises in step with this change. The gain over the 30-day index and over GB, by contrast, holds in every period, so the value of matching the maturity to the horizon does not depend on market conditions.
The remaining rows change how we measure the two sides of the comparison. Implied variances from mark prices, from the two-sided measure, from the last 15 minutes of the window, from narrower or wider strike bands, from volume-weighted medians, or from days on which the futures price moved less than 1 percent during the window all give gains close to the main row. So do realized variances from the spot market, from the Deribit perpetual contract, from one-minute returns, from the average of five offset five-minute grids, from a realized kernel, and from a measure that ends with the delivery price, which Deribit computes as an average of the index between 07:30 and 08:00. Each of these checks answers a specific concern. Mark and two-sided prices remove the effect of the side that starts the trade. The calm-window test drops days on which the price moved during the measurement hour, so the gain does not come from options that simply see a move that has already started. The realized-variance variants show that the result does not depend on how we treat noise in the price record or on the exact rule for the final price.
The last row is a placebo. If we replace the one-day implied variance with the value from the previous morning, the gain over HAR becomes a loss of 37.4 percent for bitcoin and 34.8 percent for ether, and the gain over GB disappears. The information in one-day options is fresh, and it is lost within a day. This fast decay is what we should expect if the shortest options measure the current level of volatility rather than a slow-moving average, and it means that a desk must read the option price each morning rather than reuse yesterday’s value.
Panel B relates log realized variance to the log forecast of the strongest historical model and on the log of an option-based forecast. If option prices only repeated what past returns already show, the coefficient on the option-based forecast would be zero. With the raw one-day implied variance, the coefficient is 0.65 for bitcoin and 0.61 for ether, both significant at the 1 percent level, against about 0.4 for the historical forecast. With IV+HAR, the coefficient on the option-based forecast rises to 0.85 and 0.88, and the strongest historical model adds nothing significant. In plain terms, once a forecaster knows the one-day option price, past returns add almost nothing, while the reverse is not true. The one-day implied variance is also close to unbiased in logs. Relating log realized variance to log IV alone gives slopes of 1.00 and 0.97, and for ether, the joint test of a zero intercept and a unit slope does not reject. The option price is therefore not only related to what follows but is on the right scale, so a desk can use it directly, after at most the small correction for the average gap that IV-C applies.

3.4 Matching maturity to the forecast horizon
The 30-day index fails because its maturity does not match the forecast horizon. Table 4 makes this point directly. We forecast the realized variance from 09:00 over the next h days, for h of 1, 2, 3, 7, 14, and 30, with the at-the-money implied variance of each maturity k defined in the data section and with the 30-day index. Each option measure is scaled from k to h days and then calibrated on a rolling window, as IV-C is, and each cell reports the gain over a HAR model for the same horizon. For each horizon, all measures are compared on the same days. Along the diagonal, the option maturity matches the horizon. For horizons of one, two, three, and seven days, the matching maturity has the lowest loss of all seven option measures in both markets. Its gain over HAR is 14.2, 12.6, 7.8, and 2.9 percent for bitcoin, and 9.9, 8.7, 9.6, and 0.1 percent for ether. Maturities shorter than the horizon miss the days that they do not cover, and longer maturities mix in days that the forecast does not need, and both lose accuracy with the distance from the diagonal. The 30-day index, used at a one-day horizon, is 32.1 and 23.3 percent worse than HAR.
The economic logic is simple. Each option prices the variance up to its own expiry and nothing beyond. On the diagonal, the option covers exactly the days that the forecast needs. Off the diagonal, the forecaster has to stretch or shrink the horizon of the option, and this mixes in days with a different expected variance. The result is consistent with Gruber, Tebaldi and Trojani (2021), who show that short-term and long-term volatility are separate factors in the implied volatility surface. A single number cannot carry both factors, so a 30-day figure scaled to one day must miss the short-term one. For practice, the lesson is that a risk manager should match the expiry to the holding period, and in a market with daily expiries such a match is always available.
The pattern weakens at 14 and 30 days. There, the seven-day or 14-day maturity has the lowest loss, and no option measure beats HAR significantly. At these horizons, the slow-moving part of volatility dominates, and past returns already describe it well, so option prices add less. When the option measure is added to the HAR model instead, the matching maturity ranks first or second at every horizon up to seven days, and the gains are significant at short horizons for every maturity up to a week.

4. What One-Day Variance Costs
4.1 The level and term structure of the variance premium
We turn from information to price. Panel A of Table 5 reports the variance premium, in volatility points, for at-the-money options with maturities from 1 to 30 days, measured at traded prices. Standard errors are robust to heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. On the median day, the one-day implied volatility is above the realized volatility in both markets, by 2.7 points for bitcoin and 0.5 points for ether, and the premium is positive on 59 and 51 percent of days. The mean tells a different story. It is -1.4 points for bitcoin, significant only at the 10 percent level, and -4.8 points for ether, significant at the 1 percent level. The log ratio of implied to realized variance, which weighs each day less by its size, is positive for bitcoin and negative for ether.
The gap between the median and the mean is the main fact of this section. A seller of one-day variance at the money is paid on more than half of the days, but the payments are small, while the losses on the worst days are large. The average therefore depends on a few days, and the typical day gives a misleading picture of the trade.
Panel B uses the mark price of the exchange instead of the traded price. Because most trades take place below the mark, which we discuss later, the premium is higher at the mark. The mean one-day premium is 0.3 points for bitcoin, which is not different from zero, and -2.6 points for ether. The median rises to 4.1 and 2.7 points. Across maturities, the at-the-money premium is about flat in volatility points (Figure 2). On days when both maturities are available, the one-day premium in annualized variance is below the 30-day premium at traded prices in both markets, significant at the 1 and 5 percent levels, and the difference keeps its sign at mark prices.
A flat term structure is unusual by the standards of stock markets. There, the price of variance risk is largest in absolute value at short maturities and falls with maturity, and selling volatility is the most profitable option strategy for both retail and institutional traders. Crypto options at the money show no such steep short end. One reading is that one-day crypto options are priced mainly by traders who sell volatility as a source of income, so the at-the-money price leaves little room for a premium, a point we return to in Table 9.
The gap between the median and the mean points to a few days on which the realized variance is far above the implied variance. Panel D confirms this in variance units. The mean premium for bitcoin is negative over all days, becomes much smaller when we drop the 1 percent of days with the highest realized variance, and is no longer different from zero when we drop the top 5 percent. At mark prices, the bitcoin premium turns significantly positive once the top 5 percent of days is dropped. For ether, the mean remains negative after the same trimming at traded prices, but it loses most of its size. Figure 3 shows the same fact in another way. It sorts the days from the highest to the lowest realized variance and adds up the premium. The sum falls steeply over the first few percent of days, keeps falling until about one quarter of the days are included, and rises slowly afterward. The 5 percent of days with the highest realized variance account for 109 percent of the total negative sum for bitcoin and 89 percent for ether.
The whole average variance premium is decided by about one day in twenty. For a seller, the expected income and the risk of large losses come from different days, so a short record of steady gains says little about the true return of the strategy. The frequent jumps in bitcoin prices and the evidence that downside risk is priced in crypto returns (Zhang et al., 2021) point in the same direction: in this market, the tails carry most of the risk and most of the price of risk.
At-the-money options leave out the price of protection against large moves. Panel C repeats the calculation with the model-free implied variance, which adds out-of-the-money options. The model-free variance is about 25 percent above the at-the-money variance at one day and 27 percent at 30 days for bitcoin, with similar values for ether. Part of this gap comes from the coarse strike grid. When we apply the same formula to prices that we generate from a flat volatility on the traded strikes, the grid alone produces a ratio of 1.055 at one day and 1.083 at 30 days for bitcoin. We remove this part day by day, and Panel C reports the premium after this correction. It is positive at every maturity in both markets. At one day, it is 3.8 points for bitcoin and 1.9 points for ether, significant at the 1 and 5 percent levels, and at 30 days, it is 5.2 and 5.4 points. In variance units, however, the one-day model-free premium is not different from zero in either market (Panel D), because the same extreme days dominate the mean. A seller of one day of variance is paid on about seven days out of ten and gives the payment back on the rest. These results place the compensation for one-day variance risk in the out-of-the-money options and in a small set of days. The at-the-money option, which most trades use, is priced close to the variance that follows.
Where the premium sits has an economic meaning. Buyers of out-of-the-money options pay for protection against large moves, and the sellers of this protection earn the premium. In crypto, demand from volatility and directional traders is related to the prices of out-of-the-money options, as we discuss with Table 9, which is consistent with a premium that sits in the wings. The model-free premium is positive in volatility points but not in variance units, so the sellers of tail protection are paid on most days and give much of this back on the few days when the protection is needed. For an investor, the practical point is that the price of one-day variance depends on which options are used to measure it, and the at-the-money straddle, the most traded structure, is close to fair.

4.2 Continuous and jump components
Table 6 splits the premium into two parts to show which part of the option price carries the compensation. The center part compares the at-the-money implied variance with the part of the realized variance that comes from ordinary price moves. The tail part compares the extra variance in the model-free price, after the strike-grid correction, with the part of the realized variance that comes from jumps. The two parts add up to the model-free premium. The center part is positive on about two days in three, but its mean is negative. At mark prices, it is not significant at any maturity, and at traded prices, it is significantly negative at maturities of up to three days, because most trades take place below the mark. The tail part is positive at every maturity in both markets and at both prices, and it is significant at the 1 percent level. Per day of maturity, the tail premium at 30 days is about twice the one-day premium. The total premium is not different from zero at any maturity, because the negative mean of the center part offsets the tail premium. The price of the center of the distribution is therefore close to fair at every maturity, and the compensation that the market pays for variance risk comes from the price of the tails.
Table 7 asks on which days each part is earned. We mark a day as a jump day when the standard ratio test of bipower to realized variance rejects no jump at the 1 percent level, which happens on 18 percent of days for bitcoin and 19 percent for ether. The tail part is positive on days without jumps and negative on jump days, in both markets. A seller of tail protection is paid a small amount on most days and pays out on the days when a jump occurs. The center part shows the opposite pattern, and its losses come from very volatile days without a jump. Only about one in six of the 5 percent of days with the highest realized variance is a jump day for bitcoin, and about one in ten for ether, so the days that drive the negative mean in Figure 3 are mostly days of high continuous volatility. The price of the tails on the previous morning does not separate jump days from other days. A probit forecast based on it has an area under the curve close to one half in both markets. The tail premium is not larger on days that contain a release of US inflation or payroll data or a Federal Reserve decision, and the total premium on these days and on weekends is not different from zero.
The split between the two parts depends on how we separate jumps from continuous moves. When we use truncated realized variance, which assigns more of each large move to jumps, the tail part turns negative and the center part moves close to zero (last row of Table 7). The total premium does not change, so this choice moves the compensation between the two parts but leaves the conclusion that one-day variance is close to fairly priced unchanged.

5. Trading the One-Day Straddle
5.1 The hedged straddle and trading costs
The variance premium compares two variances. A trader earns something different, namely the price of an option minus the cost of hedging it. We therefore follow a seller of the at-the-money straddle. Each day, she sells the call and the put of the series that expires the next morning, at the strike closest to the futures price. In the base case, she trades at the last traded price of each option in the window. She hedges with the same perpetual futures contract every hour, with the Black delta at the implied volatility of the entry price, and she holds the position to expiry. We report the result in US dollars per 10,000 dollars of the underlying. The result of the buyer is the negative of the result of the seller before costs, and both pay the costs. We apply a one-way cost of 0, 2, 5, or 10 basis points to the notional of each option trade and each hedge trade. Days without a qualifying straddle count as zero. Two further entry rules bound the prices at which a trader can deal. Under the own-side rule, the seller enters at the last seller-initiated trade price of each leg and the buyer at the last buyer-initiated trade price, which is roughly what each side pays when she demands immediacy. Under the mark rule, both enter at the mark price recorded with the last trade, which is a benchmark near the middle of the spread rather than a price at which anyone traded.
Panel A of Table 8 reports the mean result. At the last traded price and before costs, the bitcoin seller earns 5.8 dollars per day, significant at the 5 percent level. This is 2.8 percent of the average premium of 209 dollars, so almost all of the premium pays for the hedge (Panel B). The ether seller loses 4.3 dollars per day, which is not different from zero. Costs change the picture quickly. At a one-way cost of two basis points, the seller and the buyer both lose in both markets. Panel C reports the one-way cost at which the mean result is zero. The bitcoin seller breaks even at about 1.1 basis point, and the ether seller is below zero before any cost.
These numbers show how thin the margin of a one-day seller is. Of every 100 dollars of premium, about 97 pay for the hedge. A break-even cost of about one basis point is below the basic taker fees of the main crypto venues, which are a few basis points of the notional for each option and futures trade, so most traders would lose money with this strategy. This differs from stock markets, where writing delta-hedged options earns returns that survive realistic costs and where option strategies chosen by machine learning stay profitable after costs. At the one-day horizon in crypto, the premium is simply too small to pay for the trading that it requires.
The entry price matters as much as the cost. A seller who trades at her own side of the spread loses 1.8 dollars per day in bitcoin, which is not different from zero, and 9.8 dollars in ether, significant at the 1 percent level, before any fee. A buyer at her own side also loses in both markets. At the mark price, the bitcoin seller would earn 10.5 dollars per day before costs, and the ether seller would break even. The difference between the mark rule and the own-side rule, 12 dollars per day in bitcoin and 10 dollars in ether, is the cost of crossing the spread, and it is larger than the premium that a seller earns at the last traded price. Changing the hedge changes little. Hedging every 15 minutes, hedging with the Deribit perpetual contract, or computing the delta from the HAR or IV-C forecast moves the daily result of the bitcoin seller by less than one dollar, and none of these changes is significant. Hedging every four hours gives a lower result, significantly so for ether.
The cost of crossing the spread is therefore the largest single item in the economics of the trade. This is in line with stock market evidence that option spreads are wide relative to the price impact of trades (Kaeck, van Kervel & Seeger, 2022) and that market makers widen spreads when faster traders can pick off their quotes. The small effect of the hedge frequency also matters for practice. Alexander and Imeraj (2023) show that bitcoin implied volatility curves behave differently from equity curves, which makes the choice of hedge ratio less settled in crypto. Our results suggest that at a one-day horizon, the hedge rule is a second-order concern next to the price at which the position is opened.
The positive result of the bitcoin seller at the last traded price may seem at odds with the negative mean premium in Table 5. It is not. The premium is a difference of variances, and variance grows with the square of the price move. The payoff of a straddle grows in proportion to the move, and a hedged straddle becomes less sensitive to further moves once the price is far from the strike. The most volatile days therefore weigh less in the result of the seller than in the variance premium, and her result is closer to the median premium than to the mean. These days still matter. Her worst day costs 1,212 dollars in bitcoin and 1,727 dollars in ether, several times the premium of a normal day, and the daily result has a skewness of -3.2 and -3.1.

5.2 Traded prices and order flow
Table 9 looks at who sets the traded prices. In the at-the-money trades of our window, the seller initiates 77 percent of the bitcoin trades and 82 percent of the ether trades. Seller-initiated trades take place at an implied volatility 2.5 points below the mark of the exchange for bitcoin and 3.3 points below it for ether, while buyer-initiated trades take place almost at the mark (Panel A). The flow in one-day options thus comes mainly from traders who sell volatility and accept a price below the mark to do so. This matters for how we read the premium, but it does not change its sign. Panel B computes the one-day premium separately from buyer-initiated and seller-initiated trades. The premium is lower at seller-initiated prices, as expected, yet it is negative on average at both sets of prices. Panel C reports the same quantities by year. The share of seller-initiated trades rises to between 81 and 91 percent in 2022 to 2024 and falls to about two thirds in 2025 and 2026, and the gap between traded and mark prices narrows from about three points in 2022 to less than one point. The bitcoin seller earns a positive result before costs in each year from 2020 to 2024 and a negative one in 2025 and 2026. The narrower gap does not help her, because the premium at mark prices falls over the same years.
Who are these sellers? Our data do not identify them, but the pattern fits the growth of yield products in crypto, such as covered call strategies and structured notes, which sell short-dated options on a schedule and care more about a steady income than about the price of each trade. With this flow, trade prices sit below the mark, and the market makers who take the other side are paid for supplying liquidity. Market makers set their prices with their own inventory and hedging costs in mind. The narrower gap after 2024 is consistent with more competition among liquidity providers as the market grew.
Two implications follow. First, comparisons of implied and realized variance that use traded prices understate the price of variance whenever the flow is one-sided, so a study of variance premia should state the price it uses. Second, because the premium is negative at both buyer-initiated and seller-initiated prices, our main conclusion that the one-day at-the-money premium is small does not depend on which side starts the trade.

6. One-Day Options and Risk Limits
A desk that holds a position overnight, or an exchange that sets margins for the next day, needs a quantile of the next-day return rather than a variance. We therefore turn each variance forecast into a value at risk for a long and a short position in the perpetual futures contract from 09:00 to 08:00 the next day,

,(7)

Where,  is the empirical quantile of past returns divided by the square root of their forecasts over the previous 500 days, taken in the lower tail for a long position and in the upper tail for a short position. The expected shortfall is the mean of the standardized returns beyond the quantile, scaled in the same way. We check the coverage of the 99 percent value at risk with the unconditional coverage test of Kupiec and the conditional coverage test of Christoffersen, and we test the expected shortfall with the residual test of McNeil and Frey, which asks whether losses beyond the value at risk exceed the expected shortfall on average. It is a simple relative of the regression-based backtests of Bayer and Dimitriadis (2022). To compare the capital that each forecast requires, we scale every forecast so that it has the same number of exceedances over the full evaluation period and report the average margin that results.
Table 10 reports the results. The GB forecast gives the thinnest limits, and they are too thin. Its 99 percent value at risk is exceeded on 1.84 percent of days for a long bitcoin position and 1.75 percent for a long ether position, and both tests reject its coverage. The HAR model passes for bitcoin but not for a short ether position, where its limit is exceeded on 1.75 percent of days. The forecasts that use the one-day implied variance pass both tests in three of the four cases. The exception is the same short ether position, with an exceedance rate of 1.69 percent for IV-C, which is rejected at the 5 percent level but not at the 1 percent level. The expected shortfall test rejects only GB, for long positions. The last column compares capital at equal coverage. An option-based forecast requires the lowest margin in three of the four cases, for example 6.59 percent of the notional for a long bitcoin position against 6.73 percent for HAR, and 8.88 percent for a short ether position against 10.25 percent for HAR. For a long ether position, GB requires the least once it is scaled to the correct coverage. The gains are modest, because the tails of daily returns are fat whatever the variance forecast, but they point in the same direction as the forecast comparison.
The economic value of these differences is easiest to see in margin terms. For a short ether position, a limit built on IV-C needs 8.88 percent of the notional against 10.25 percent for HAR at the same coverage, which frees about 13 percent of the capital that the position ties up. For an exchange or a broker that sets margins for many accounts, such a saving is large, and it comes without more breaches. This is in line with Lyócsa, Plíhal and Výrost (2024), who find that implied volatility improves day-ahead expected shortfall forecasts for the euro-dollar rate, most of all in uncertain periods, and with Bernard, Perchiazzo and Vanduffel (2024), who show how option prices can be turned into value at risk directly.
The failure of GB is also instructive. A flexible model can fit the center of the distribution well and still miss its tails, which is why tail forecasts call for tools built for quantiles (Buse, Görgen & Schienle, 2025). Option-based limits avoid part of this problem because the option price already reflects the market view of how large the next move can be. For a risk manager, the message is that the one-day option price is a cheap and timely input for daily limits, and that a model that wins on average accuracy is not automatically the best choice for the tails.

7. Conclusion
One-day options on bitcoin and ether are informative and close to fairly priced. Their implied variance forecasts next-day variance better than past returns, including machine learning models, and far better than a one-month index, and the most accurate option-based forecast at each horizon up to a week comes from the expiry that matches it. Implied variance is above realized variance on most days, the premium disappears on average because of a small set of very volatile days, and what remains is smaller than the gap between traded and mark prices and smaller than the cost of trading. Split into parts, the center of the option price is close to the ordinary variance that follows, and the price of the tails earns a small premium on calm days that it gives back on days with jumps. The results suggest four points for practice. A desk that prices, hedges, or margins a position for the next day should use the one-day implied variance and should not scale down a one-month figure. A seller of one-day options should not expect the premium that is seen at longer maturities, her risk is concentrated in a few days, and her result depends more on where she trades in the spread than on how she hedges. Comparisons of implied and realized variance at short horizons should state whether they use traded, mark, or model-free prices, because the three can give different signs. And limits built on one-day implied variance pass standard backtests with less capital than limits built on past returns in most cases. Our evidence comes from two cryptocurrencies on one exchange and from traded prices without quotes, and whether the same holds for daily-expiry options on equity indexes is a question for future work.
